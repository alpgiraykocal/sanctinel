About & methodology

Sanctions Search screens names, companies, vessels, aircraft and identifiers against six issuing authorities in one search:

  • the financial-sanctions lists of the U.S. Treasury (OFAC), the European Union, the UN Security Council and the UK (OFSI);
  • the U.S. export-control restricted-party lists of the Commerce Department (BIS) and the State Department.

It adds multilingual fuzzy matching, identifier screening, cross-authority linking and 50 Percent Rule ownership chains. It is an analysis tool: a hit is a lead to verify, not a determination, and it is not a system of record for compliance decisions.

How the data is built

  • Nightly rebuild. All six authorities are fetched every night at 23:00 UTC. The result is committed with the code, so the search, the insights and the change log are always computed from the same snapshot. If any source failed or was carried forward, a recovery run at 11:00 UTC tries again; on a clean day it does nothing.
  • One source failing does not stop the others. If a source, or a single OFAC list, cannot be fetched, its records are carried forward from the last good build. The rest of the data still updates on time. A carried-forward source is reported with the date of the data it holds.
  • Coverage guard. A build that would lose an authority the previous snapshot had is refused, and the complete snapshot stays in place. A list that silently gets shorter produces false negatives, so this is treated as an error, not a warning.
  • Field-level quality gate. Every build measures how complete each field is, per authority, and compares the result with the last published build. See how quality is checked.
  • What gets published.
    • The snapshot, as one file per authority.
    • The change list: who was added and who was removed.
    • The quality profile.
    • An append-only history of every build's changes, which What changed reads.
    • The whole snapshot as a downloadable release asset.
  • Failures raise an alarm. A failed or degraded build opens an issue in the repository, naming what failed. The issue closes itself on the next clean run.
  • On disk, the data stays as published. Labels, spellings and country names are kept exactly as each authority wrote them, because they are what an analyst cites. The fixes described next are applied when the snapshot is loaded, so they reach the data even on a night a source is down.

What is done to the data

The fixes below correct defects without rewriting what a publisher said. Each one is idempotent, tested, and measured by the quality gate.

Everything the feeds publish is read

A parser only reads what it asks for. Each feed is therefore inventoried field by field against what the parsers extract (see quality). That inventory found fields that had been silently dropped. All of them are read now:

  • OFAC:
    • street, city, region and postcode of every address;
    • issue and expiry dates of identity documents, the issuing authority and location, and OFAC's own invalid flag;
    • the reliability grade of each feature (Confirmed, Reported or False);
    • remarks, titles, and the names of related parties.
  • EU:
    • email, phone, website and fax (published inside the address);
    • gender, every function and title, and every remark;
    • the designation details and Official Journal links;
    • identity-document dates and issuers, with the known-false, revoked, lost and expired flags;
    • year-range and Hijri birth dates.
  • UN: the name in its original script (mostly Arabic), titles and designations, approximate birth dates, document dates, places of issue and notes, and INTERPOL-UN notice links.
  • UK:
    • The labelled fields OFSI packs into one free-text column become separate fields: list reference, statement of reasons, UN reference, company registration number, IMO number, phone, email, website, parent company, subsidiaries and vessel details.
    • Passports and national IDs become documents, kept with OFSI's own description of them.

Defects corrected on read

  • Dates. Listing dates become yyyy-mm-dd (UK dates are day-first, U.S. dates month-first). Sorting, the timeline and the change log compare them as text.
  • Exact repeats. Repeated addresses, attributes, identifiers and aliases are dropped. Different published spellings of a name are kept.
  • Formatting debris. Doubled and non-breaking spaces, invisible bidirectional marks, and stray leading or trailing punctuation from list splitting are removed.
  • Numbered values. A field holding (1) MM611523 (2) EE947317 becomes one identifier per number, so each one matches on its own.
  • Name scripts. Each name is labelled with the script it is actually written in.
  • Party type. The Consolidated Screening List carries no person/entity flag. A BIS or State listing whose name contains a legal form or organisation word (LTD, LLC, GMBH, JSC, INSTITUTE…) is classed as an entity. Every other one stays “Listed party”: nothing else is guessed.
  • Country names. Country wordings from the four feeds (ISO codes, “Iran (Islamic Republic of)”, “Korea, North”, demonyms, “Region: Gaza”, multi-country fields) resolve to one jurisdiction for matching and statistics. The published wording stays on the record.

What the sources say about their own data

  • Flagged documents. A document the source flags as invalid, expired, revoked, lost or false keeps that flag on the record card. It still matches, because it is often the document the party actually uses, but the match explanation names its status.
  • Values marked false. A value its publisher marks false (OFAC's reliability grade) stays on the record, struck through. It is never used as evidence by the year-of-birth and country modifiers or by cross-listing. A value that is only reported, not confirmed, is labelled as such.
  • Lapsed BIS denial orders. A Denied Persons order whose end date has passed is still published by the Consolidated Screening List. Such a record is marked expired, on its card and in its sanctions type, and search ranks it below every live restriction. It is not deleted, because it is history a reviewer may need.

How matching works

Every query is compared with each party's primary name, every alias, the name in its own script, and its identifiers. Names are normalized first:

  • case and accents are removed;
  • Cyrillic, Greek, Arabic and Hebrew are transliterated;
  • Chinese, Japanese and Korean are matched as characters;
  • legal-form and descriptor words are recognised across languages (GmbH, OOO, ТОВ, Sh.p.k., Sdn Bhd…).

A person's name and an organisation's are read differently. For a company, descriptor words are dropped (“Trading”, “Group”, “SA”). For an individual, only name particles (al-, el-, de, da, la…) and lone initials are. Many short descriptor words are also common parts of personal names: SA, MA, YU, HAN, SOE, ONG. Dropping them turned “Soe Win” into just “Win”, which matched every listed “… Win”. A party of unknown kind is read both ways.

Each pair of words is then scored by an ensemble, and the best signal wins:

  • Jaro-Winkler: typos and shared prefixes.
  • Damerau-Levenshtein: edit distance, counting an adjacent swap as one edit. Confusable characters (0/O, 1/L) cost half.
  • Sørensen-Dice and trigram Jaccard: character overlap, robust to insertions and reordering.
  • Metaphone: sound-alike spellings (Mohammed and Muhammad, Philip and Filip).
  • Transliteration folding: romanization variants (Kadyrov and Kadirov, Yousef and Yusuf), including the German, French and Swedish forms the EU publishes (Tschernow, Tchernov, Setjin for Sechin).
  • Acronyms and initials: “CML” matches Caspian Maritime Logistics, and “J.” matches John.
  • Rarity weighting: a match on a rare surname counts far more than one on “Company” or “Mohammed”.
  • Homoglyph defence: Cyrillic look-alike letters inside a Latin word are folded back, so “Gazprоm” screens like “Gazprom”.
  • Misspelled descriptors: a query word one edit from a legal form (“Tradng”, “Limted”) is also scored without that word. A query word one edit from a word that is normalized (“Tehcnology” for Technology) is read as that word.
  • One-keystroke typos: one wrong, missing or extra letter in a word of six or more letters (“Mohnen” for Mohsen), or two adjacent letters swapped (“Zhnag” for Zhang), is scored as a near-match. The first letter must be the same.
  • Arabic particles and honorifics: al- and el- are the same article, attached or not (“Alghabra”, “al-Ghabra”). Abd, Abdel and Abdoul are read as Abdul. Haji, Sheikh, Mullah, Maulavi, and military ranks before a person's name, are read as titles.
  • Word spacing: words written together or apart match (“Abdulrahman” and “Abdul Rahman”, “Alek sandr”). They score just below the name as written.

Words are paired one-to-one regardless of order, so “Mohammed Ali” matches “Ali Mohammed”. A one-word query that exactly matches one word of a longer name scores 0.96: above the default threshold, below a full-name match. Each result shows which words matched, and by which method.

Threshold and completeness

  • Threshold. The default threshold is 0.95. At and above it, search uses a candidate index proven to return every party a full scan would; a CI check verifies this on every change to the matcher.
  • Below-the-line check. Lowering the threshold is done with the below-the-line check. It scans the whole list down to 0.80, shows how many hits each step would add, and lists the parties just under the line, plus any name that literally starts with the query.
  • Result limits. Results show the top 200 hits, and the true total is always reported.

Corroboration and ranking

  • Year of birth and country are optional. A matching year adds 0.05 and a contradicting one subtracts 0.06; a matching country adds 0.04 and a contradicting one subtracts 0.04. Ranges, “circa” and Hijri dates are respected, a territory corroborates its sovereign state, and values a source marks false are ignored.
  • Ties are disclosed. When several hits share the top score, the page says so, and it says what separates them (date of birth, nationality, country). Their order is stable, but it is not a ranking.
  • Lapsed restrictions rank last, after every live one.

Identifiers

An exact match on any of these is reported as an identifier hit with score 1.00:

  • passport, national ID, tax ID or company registration number;
  • IMO number, MMSI or call sign;
  • aircraft tail or serial number;
  • LEI, ISIN or SWIFT/BIC;
  • email, or a digital-currency address.

Comparison ignores case, spaces and punctuation. Identifier types are classified across the four authorities' wordings. Some generic types (“Identification Number”, “Other identification number”) are named more precisely by the issuing authority or a remark, for example INN or OGRN; that more precise name is used. A country name filed as an identity number by mistake is never treated as one.

When a query looks like an identifier, the below-the-line panel says how it was read (IMO, MMSI, crypto address…), and how many parties in the snapshot carry that kind of identifier. A miss on a kind few parties carry is then not mistaken for a clearance.

Ownership & the 50 Percent Rule

OFAC publishes relationships between listed parties. They drive a graph implementing OFAC's 50 Percent Rule: an entity owned 50% or more, alone or in aggregate, by blocked persons is itself blocked even when it is not listed.

  • Blocked owners. Each result says whether its ownership chain reaches a blocked owner, how many hops away the nearest one is, and whether several blocked owners together (the aggregate case) may cross the threshold.
  • Ownership structures. Results that belong to the same ownership structure are grouped and labelled, so a long result list reads as a few corporate groups rather than as unrelated parties.
  • Two network views.
    • Radial rings every relationship by hop distance.
    • Hierarchy stacks principals above subordinates. Direction is resolved per relationship type: OFAC phrases relationships outward from the listed party (“Owned or Controlled By”, “Providing support to”).
  • Which links count. Only ownership links count for the rule. They are drawn solid red and counted separately from support, agency, office and family links.

OFAC publishes no ownership percentages, so the 50% threshold itself cannot be computed from list data. The chain is a lead to confirm against corporate-registry or KYC records. The rule is OFAC doctrine; the EU, UN and UK apply their own ownership-and-control tests.

Cross-listing

The authorities designate independently, so one shipowner can appear four times under four spellings. Listings of the same real party are linked, so a result says “also listed by the EU and the UK”. They are never merged: obligations differ by regime, so each record stands as its authority published it.

  • Shared identifier, high confidence. The listings share a document number or a UN Security Council reference (the reference the EU and UK publish on the listings that implement a UN designation), and their names share a rare word. Tax-office and other shared codes do not count.
  • Same name and overlapping birth year, high confidence. This applies to people.
  • Same company name and a shared jurisdiction, medium confidence. This applies only to entities. A personal name alone never links two records.

Precision is preferred over recall: a wrong link would tell a reviewer that two different people are one.

How quality is checked

  • Field-level quality gate, on every build. Per authority it measures:
    • the share of records with aliases, an address, an address with more than its country, an identifier, a birth date and a nationality;
    • the number of ownership edges.
    Across the whole snapshot it measures the share of listing dates in ISO form, of birth dates the parser reads, of country values it resolves, and of identifier types it can classify.
  • Two outcomes.
    • A build materially worse than the last published one is a regression: it is not published, and the regressed authority is carried forward.
    • A build that is merely below a fixed floor publishes, and ends red so it is noticed.
    Floors are written in code, so moving one is a reviewable change. A genuine change by a publisher can be accepted for a single run, by hand.
  • Source inventory and parse check. A read-only CI workflow lists every field each live feed publishes. It also builds the snapshot from the live feeds in memory and reports what the parsers extract. A parser change is checked against real data before it is merged.
  • Tests.
    • Unit tests cover the parsers, the normalization, the matcher, the quality gate and the server.
    • Browser tests cover every page, in light and dark themes, including an automated WCAG 2 AA accessibility audit.
    • A recall check proves the fast search path returns everything a full scan would.
  • Live numbers. The current numbers are on Insights → Data quality & sources.

    Using the results

    • Every card names the operative restriction for its authority and list: block and freeze, asset freeze, rejection, a securities or correspondent-account restriction, a licence requirement, or a denial of export privileges.
    • Every record has a permalink. Its page can copy the full record as JSON. Each hit prints as a screening memo. The result set exports as CSV, recording the query, threshold, snapshot and authorities screened, and each hit's score and reasoning.
    • What changed lists who entered and left the lists in each build. It is the rescreening trigger for an existing book.
    • Insights describes what is inside the snapshot, and how complete and current it is.

    Privacy & security

    • Search text is never stored or logged. Queries are processed in memory. The access log records the path and status of a request, not its query.
    • No third parties. The page loads nothing from any other site: no analytics, no trackers, and fonts are served locally. A strict content-security policy enforces this.
    • Rate limits. Requests are rate-limited per client: 120 a minute, and 30 a minute for searches.

    Limitations

    • A hit is a lead to verify, not a determination, and the absence of a hit is not a clearance. Confirm anything material against the issuing authority's own list.
    • Regimes differ. An OFAC blocking designation, an EU or UN asset freeze, a UK designation and a BIS or State export-control listing carry different legal obligations. An export-control listing means a licence requirement or loss of export privileges, not an asset freeze. Never carry OFAC doctrine across to another regime.
    • The snapshot can lag. It may be up to a day behind an authority, longer for a source that is being carried forward. The freshness table above shows which.
    • Gaps in the sources themselves.
      • State Department lists carry no addresses, and the ITAR Debarred List carries no listing dates.
      • Some OFAC addresses are published with no content at all, and are shown as “address on file”.
      • Some OFAC records carry no legal authority.
      None of these is inferred.
    • Cross-listing is conservative, so some real matches across authorities stay unlinked.
    • Not covered: other national regimes, or the EU dual-use export-control list.
