How networks are formed — and what they are not

OFAC publishes links between the parties it lists: who owns or controls whom, who acts for, supports or leads whom, and who is family of whom. Followed across the whole list, those links form networks. This page lists them and draws each one.

  • A network is a group of listed parties joined by published links, directly or through each other. Pairs (two parties and one link) are counted but not listed.
  • One group is too large to read: about 2,700 parties around Iran, Hizballah and their facilitators, chained together by “providing support to” links. It is divided into communities that are far more densely linked inside than to each other (the Louvain method). Each community is listed as a network of its own, and the links to its neighbouring communities are shown.
  • Each network is named after its hub: the most strongly linked party in it. Ownership links count double, because ownership is what the 50 Percent Rule reaches.
  • Sorting. Largest; most ownership links (where derivative blocking is most likely); most members also listed by the EU, UN or UK; or most recent designation.
  • Only OFAC publishes relationships, so the networks are OFAC's. A member's cross-listings by other authorities are shown on it.
  • A network shows that an authority linked these parties, not that they act together today. It is a display and triage aid, not a determination. See how relationships are used.
